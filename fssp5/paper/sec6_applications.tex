\section{Applications to Four and Five States}\label{sec:applications}

We now combine the necessary conditions with satisfiability solvers. Each
statement of this section rests on one or more of four kinds of evidence, which
we state for each result and summarize in \cref{tab:results}: proofs in the
text; exact finite computations, that is, simulations and exhaustive
enumerations that decide a finite statement completely; satisfiable formulas,
whose models we check by simulation; and unsatisfiable formulas, certified by
proofs that independent checkers verify. Statements that rest on finite
simulations alone are observations about the simulated ranges. We mark them as
such, and no proof uses them. The theorem, the propositions and the corollary
of this section are marked computer-assisted: their proofs use computations,
whereas the results of \cref{sec:lines,sec:right,sec:left,sec:early} are
proved without computation.

\subsection{Method}\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Encodings}
The formula $\MT_k(\mathcal N;M,N')$ (MT for minimal time) has one Boolean
variable for each possible value of each entry of a $k$-state rule table, of
each cell of $C_\infty$ with $t+i\le M$, and of each cell of $R_n$ with
$n\in\mathcal N$; clauses require that exactly one value is chosen for each
entry and each cell. Its clauses state the local rule, that no
half-line cell is $\Fq$, that the line of length $n$ is synchronized at time
$2n-2$ for every $n\in\mathcal N$, the return chains of \cref{lem:return} for
$n,n+1\in\mathcal N$, and $\PUMP(n,n')$ for $4\le n<n'\le N'$; the formula
$\MT_k(\mathcal N;M)$ has no pumping clauses. The formula $\G_k(T,N_1,C)$ is
encoded in the same way, with $M=2N_1$; its regions are closed under
predecessors, so that the table determines every encoded cell.
\Cref{app:encoding} gives the clauses and shows that the encoding is equivalent
to \cref{def:G}.

\paragraph{Certificates}
Every unsatisfiability claim of \cref{thm:four,prop:germs,prop:balzer} is
certified by a proof in the LRAT format~\cite{CruzFilipeEtAl17}, which is produced by the solver
CaDiCaL~\cite{CaDiCaL2} or, for one formula, obtained from a DRAT proof of the
solver Kissat~\cite{Kissat20} with \textsf{drat-trim}~\cite{WetzlerHeuleHunt14},
and verified by the independent checker \texttt{lrat-check}. Each of these
proofs is also verified by \texttt{cake\_lpr}~\cite{TanHeuleMyreen21}, a checker
that is formally verified in the theorem prover HOL4 and compiled with the
verified compiler CakeML. The eight
unsatisfiable formulas and their proofs are published with their SHA-256
digests (\cref{app:artefacts} and the data availability statement), so that
each proof can be checked against exactly its formula. The unsatisfiable
outcomes in the checks of the encodings below, where a rule table is fixed, are
predicted by simulating that rule; they are tests, not claims of their own.

\paragraph{Checks of the encodings}
A checked proof shows that a formula is unsatisfiable, but not that the formula
expresses the intended conditions; the programs that generate the formulas are
trusted. We tested them in five independent ways.
\begin{enumerate}[(1)]
\item \emph{Differential test.} A program written for this purpose from the
definitions of this paper, sharing no code with the generators, evaluates the
conditions directly by simulating the lines and the half-line: the
synchronization of the lines, the half-line up to an anti-diagonal,
$\PUMP(n,n')$ with the cone of \cref{lem:cone}, (G1)--(G5) and (B1)--(B4) of
\cref{sec:balzer}. With a rule table fixed by assumptions, each formula must be
satisfiable exactly when the table satisfies its conditions. We compared the two
on $37$ formulas (those of \cref{thm:four}, \cref{prop:sharp}~(b),
\cref{prop:germs,prop:balzer} and \cref{tab:validation}, and variants for four,
five and six states) and $3{,}684$ tables (the known rules, models of the
satisfiable formulas, the $27$ partial rules of \cref{prop:sharp}~(a) completed
at random, single and double mutations of these, and random tables): the
answers agree on all $62{,}286$ pairs, $1{,}002$ of which are satisfiable. The
formulas of \cref{thm:four,prop:balzer} in this test are identical to the
published files (\cref{app:artefacts}), and that of \cref{prop:germs} becomes
identical to its file when the selector clauses of its $230$ germs
(\cref{app:encoding}) are added. The
clauses of (B1)--(B4) alone agree with the direct check on $7{,}280$ tables, and
the cones found by search agree with \cref{lem:cone} for $2\le n<200$. No table
satisfies the formulas of \cref{thm:four}, so for them the test can only confirm
that violated conditions are detected; the same generators with other
parameters agree with the direct evaluation on satisfiable tables as well.
\item \emph{Known rules.} With the tables of Mazoyer's solution, as extracted
from Duprat's formal proof of its correctness~\cite{Duprat97}, and of
$\delta_{14}$ fixed, the formulas have the outcomes that simulation predicts
(\cref{tab:validation}).
\item \emph{Independent methods.} The formula of \cref{thm:four}~(i) and the
exhaustive enumeration of \cref{prop:sharp}~(a), a separate program, agree that
no four-state rule synchronizes the lines of lengths $2,\dots,9$. The germ
enumeration of \cref{prop:germs} and the refutations of its $14$ remaining
germs were each repeated by an independent program.
\item \emph{Models.} Every model found by a solver was re-simulated by a checker
that shares no code with the generators.
\item \emph{Second encoder.} A second program, written from the definitions of
this paper and from \cref{app:encoding} and sharing no code with the generators,
builds the eight unsatisfiable formulas again. It encodes each condition
literally: without the structural sharing of the generators, and with the cones
of the right ends found by a search over predecessors. It agrees with the direct
evaluation of~(1) on all $36{,}648$ pairs of a formula and a table, from $21$
of its formulas and $3{,}558$ tables; $643$ of these pairs are satisfiable. Its
eight formulas are unsatisfiable, with LRAT proofs verified by
\texttt{lrat-check} and \texttt{cake\_lpr}. Each
unsatisfiability claim thus rests on two formulas written by independent
programs.
\end{enumerate}
These tests make an error in the encodings unlikely, but they do not prove its
absence: a misreading of a definition shared by both encoders and the direct
evaluation would not be detected.

\paragraph{Reproducibility}
Every computational statement can be repeated with the supplementary material,
which is publicly available (see the data availability statement). It contains
the programs, the rule tables, the germ lists, the eight unsatisfiable formulas
with their SHA-256 digests, the logs, and three scripts: one writes the
formulas with the generators and compares them with the recorded digests
(about one minute); one reruns the simulations, enumerations, satisfiable
formulas and the two differential tests (about $11$ minutes); and one
regenerates every LRAT proof and checks it with \texttt{lrat-check} (and with
\texttt{cake\_lpr} if it is installed), and then
refutes the formulas of the second encoder in the same way (about two hours and
$10$\,GB). Run again with CaDiCaL~3.0.1, Kissat~4.0.4 and \textsf{drat-trim},
this script reproduced all eight recorded proofs byte for byte (its log is in
the Zenodo record of the data availability statement), so regenerated proofs
can be compared with the recorded digests.
\Cref{app:artefacts} lists each computational claim with its programs and files.

\subsection{Four States}\label{sec:four}

\begin{theorem}[computer-assisted]\label{thm:four}
No four-state rule satisfying \eqref{eq:quiescence}
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item synchronizes all lines of lengths $2,\dots,9$ in minimal time;
\item satisfies $\G_4(6,10)$: the lines of lengths $2,\dots,7$ complete and,
for the lines of lengths $8$, $9$ and $10$, the first seven rows after the
reflection, with the return chains on these rows;
\item satisfies $\G_4(4,10,10)$: the lines of lengths $2,\dots,5$ complete and,
for the lines of lengths $6,\dots,10$, the first five rows after the reflection,
with the return chains on these rows, and the right ends.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, no four-state minimal-time solution exists.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(i) Such a rule yields a model of $\MT_4(\{2,\dots,9\};16)$: a half-line cell
with $t+i\le16$ is quiescent if $i\ge10$ (\cref{lem:lightcone}) and equals a
cell of the line of length $9$ at a time $t<16$ if $i\le9$ (\cref{lem:agree}),
so it is in $W$; and the return chains hold by \cref{lem:return} for $2\le
n\le8$. This formula has $753$ variables and $17{,}605$ clauses. (ii) The
encoding of $\G_4(6,10)$ has $919$ variables and $22{,}960$ clauses. (iii) The
encoding of $\G_4(4,10,10)$ has $874$ variables and $20{,}461$ clauses. CaDiCaL
refutes each of the three formulas with an LRAT proof, and \texttt{lrat-check}
and \texttt{cake\_lpr} verify each proof. The last claim follows from (i), and also from (ii) or
(iii) with \cref{lem:G}.
\end{proof}

\Cref{fig:regions} shows the cells that the formulas of parts (ii) and (iii)
constrain, and \cref{tab:four} compares them with the weaker formulas of the
next proposition.

Part (i) certifies the bound observed by Yun\`es~\cite{Yunes93}; the earlier
proofs~\cite{Balzer67,Yunes93,Sanders94} rest on the correctness of search
programs. Parts (ii) and (iii) show how little information excludes four
states: in (iii), only the lines of lengths up to $5$ are complete. These
bounds are sharp in the following sense.

\begin{proposition}[computer-assisted]\label{prop:sharp}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item Exactly $27$ partial four-state rules (rules restricted to the
neighbourhoods they use) synchronize all lines of lengths $2,\dots,8$ in
minimal time, and no partial four-state rule synchronizes all lines of lengths
$2,\dots,9$.
\item The formulas $\G_4(5,30)$, $\G_4(6,9)$, $\G_4(4,30,9)$ and
$\G_4(3,30,12)$, the formula $\G_4(6,10)$ without (G3), and the formula that
consists of (G1) for $T=4$ and (G5) for $6\le n\le12$ are satisfiable.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) is an exhaustive enumeration. Yun\`es~\cite{Yunes93} found the same number
under the extra condition $\delta(\ast,\Lq,\Lq)=\Lq$, which is vacuous here
because none of the $27$ partial rules uses the neighbourhood $(\ast,\Lq,\Lq)$.
(b) The solver finds a model of each formula, and each model is re-simulated.
\end{proof}

In words (\cref{tab:four}): the formula of part (ii) of \cref{thm:four}
becomes satisfiable if one row is removed, even when the lines up to length $30$ are added
($\G_4(5,30)$), if the line of length $10$ is removed ($\G_4(6,9)$), or if the
return chains are removed. The formula of part (iii) becomes satisfiable if
the right end of the line of length $10$ is removed, even when the early rows
of the lines up to length $30$ are added ($\G_4(4,30,9)$), or if one row is
removed, even when the right ends of the lines up to length $12$ are added
($\G_4(3,30,12)$). The right ends alone, without the early rows and the return
chains, do not suffice either.

\begin{proposition}[computer-assisted]\label{prop:nearmiss}
Each of the five-state rules $\delta_{14}$, $\delta_{14}^{\mathrm b}$,
$\delta_{14}^{\mathrm c}$, $\delta_{14}^{\mathrm d}$ and $\delta_{14}^{\mathrm
e}$ synchronizes the line of length $n$ at time $2n-2$ for $2\le n\le14$, and
for no $n\ge15$. More precisely, for every $n\ge15$, some cell of the line of
length $n$ is $\Fq$ at a relative time $\tau\le\min(T,n-2)$, where
$T=10,14,22,12,14$, respectively.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The lines of lengths $2,\dots,60$ are simulated. For $15\le n\le60$ some cell
is $\Fq$ at a relative time $\tau\le n-2$, which proves the claim for $15\le
n<T+3$. The half-line has no cell in state $\Fq$ at the times $t<3000$, so by
\cref{lem:agree} a cell of a line of length $n\le1500$ that is $\Fq$ at a time
$t\le 2n-2$ lies in $R_n$. Simulating the half-line gives $t_0$ and $p$ that
satisfy the hypothesis of \cref{lem:certificate} for $D=2T+4\ge2T+1$, namely
$(t_0,p)=(51,8)$, $(66,3)$, $(257,192)$, $(95,48)$ and $(56,2)$. For every $n$
with $\max(15,T+3)\le n<t_0+3+p\le452$, direct simulation shows that the line
of length $n$, or of a length $n'$ in this range with $J_{n'}=J_n$ on
$[0,T+1]$, has a cell in state $\Fq$ at a relative time $\tau\le T$, hence in
$E(T)$. (One length is simulated for each class of a partition that is finer
than equality of $J_n$ on $[0,T+1]$; together with the lengths below $T+3$,
these are $35$, $39$, $413$, $116$ and $28$ lengths, the largest being $49$,
$53$, $427$, $130$ and $42$.) By \cref{lem:early,cor:reduce} with
$N_0=\max(15,T+3)$, every $R_n$ with $n\ge N_0$ has a cell of $E(T)$ in state
$\Fq$, and there $\tau\le T\le n-3$ (\cref{fig:firstfire,tab:rules}).
\end{proof}

For example, $\delta_{14}$ synchronizes the line of length $14$ at time $26$,
whereas on the line of length $15$ the cells $13$, $14$ and $15$ fire at time
$22$, eight steps after the front has reached the right end and six steps too
early (\cref{fig:delta14}). The following are observations from simulations,
valid in the stated ranges. For $3\le t<1500$ the half-line of $\delta_{14}$
takes the values $\Lq,\Gq$ at cell $1$, $\Lq,\Aq$ at cell $2$ and $\Lq,\Bq$ at
the cells $i\ge3$, and $\delta_{14}$ restricted to $\{\Lq,\Bq\}^3$ is the

barriers do not exclude this half-line in the ranges we checked: $\PUMP(n,n')$ holds for $4\le n<n'\le800$, and
\cref{lem:left} does not apply for $n\le5000$, $s\le3$ and $\ell\le24$. The
following are exact finite computations. The rules $\delta_{12}$ and
$\delta_{13}$ synchronize the lines of lengths $2,\dots,12$ and $2,\dots,13$,
respectively, and no other line of length at most $60$. Each of the seven rules
has a line of some length $14\le n\le19$ with a cell in state $\Fq$ at a
relative time $\tau\le9$, so none of them satisfies $\G_5(9,N_1)$ for
$N_1\ge19$.

\paragraph{What is new}
Synchronizing finitely many lengths is not difficult in itself: for every $k$
there is a largest $N_k\in\{1,2,\dots\}\cup\{\infty\}$ such that some $k$-state
rule synchronizes the lines of lengths $2,\dots,N_k$ in minimal time, and since
there are finitely many rules, $N_k=\infty$ exactly when a $k$-state
minimal-time solution exists. The question is how far $N_k$ reaches.
Exhaustive searches give $N_4=8$ (Yun\`es~\cite{Yunes93};
\cref{prop:sharp}~(a)), and six-state minimal-time solutions
exist~\cite{Mazoyer87}. \Cref{prop:nearmiss} gives $N_5\ge14$. We are not aware
of an earlier published bound for five states: the constructions with at most
five states that we know of synchronize restricted families of lengths, some of them in non-minimal
time~\cite{UmeoYanagihara07,Yunes08}. The rules add three things to the method
of this paper. First, a proof that five states do not suffice must use lines of
length at least $15$, or conditions other than the synchronization of the lines
of lengths $2,\dots,14$. Second, the statement that each rule fails for every
$n\ge15$ is not a finite simulation: it combines the simulation of finitely
many lengths with \cref{cor:reduce}. Third, the rules fail in the first rows
after the reflection, which is what the formulas of \cref{sec:early} target.
They are not candidates for minimal-time solutions.

\subsection{Five States: Half-Lines of Low Complexity}\label{sec:germs}

The barriers constrain the half-line, so it is natural to ask how simple the
half-line of a five-state minimal-time solution can be. We measure this by the
number of distinct neighbourhoods that it uses in a fixed region. The
\emph{germ} below $M$ of a rule is its restriction to the neighbourhoods of the
cells $(t,i)$ of its half-line with $t\ge1$ and $t+i\le M$, and its
\emph{complexity} $c_M(\delta)$ is the number of these neighbourhoods other than
$(\Lq,\Lq,\Lq)$. We use $M=78$: the half-line below the anti-diagonal $t+i=78$
contains the input anti-diagonals of all lines of lengths $n\le40$, so the germ
below $78$ determines the conditions $\PUMP(n,n')$ for $n'\le40$. For example,
$c_{78}=57$ for Mazoyer's solution, $22$ for $\delta_{12}$ and $16$ for
$\delta_{14}$ (\cref{tab:rules}).

\begin{proposition}[computer-assisted]\label{prop:germs}
Every five-state minimal-time solution $\delta$ satisfies $c_{78}(\delta)\ge15$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose that $c_{78}(\delta)\le14$. Exchanging $\Aq$ and $\Bq$ if necessary, we
may assume that $\Aq$ occurs before $\Bq$ (if at all) in the order of
anti-diagonals and, within an anti-diagonal, of time. By \eqref{eq:neverfire}
and \cref{lem:return,lem:pumping}, the germ of $\delta$ below $78$ produces no
$\Fq$, never produces $\Lq$ at the front, and satisfies $\PUMP(n,n')$ for $4\le
n<n'\le40$. A depth-first search that chooses the value of each neighbourhood
at its first occurrence enumerates all such germs, visiting about $10^8$ nodes:
there are $244$, and an independent re-implementation finds the same $244$.
For $230$ of them, the formula $\MT_5(\{2,\dots,10\};78,39)$ with the germ fixed
is unsatisfiable. A single formula covers all $230$: it has a selector variable
for each germ, clauses that make each selector fix its germ, and a clause that
requires some selector; CaDiCaL refutes it with an LRAT proof that
\texttt{lrat-check} and \texttt{cake\_lpr} verify. Each of the other $14$ germs determines
$C_\infty$ up to the anti-diagonal $3300$, and there \cref{lem:left} applies
with $s=1$: for some $n\le163$ and $\ell\le5$, the upper input anti-diagonal of
the line of length $n$ is $\ell$-periodic on $[1,Y]$ and the lower one on
$[1,Y-1]$ with $Y-1>16\ell$ (for example $n=28$, $\ell=1$ and $Y=19$,
\cref{fig:germs}(b)), as an independent simulation confirms. Hence no rule
that contains one of the $244$ germs is a minimal-time solution
(\cref{fig:germs}(a)).
\end{proof}

Complexity $15$ does occur for rules that synchronize many lines: the germs
below $78$ of $\delta_{14}^{\mathrm c}$ and $\delta_{14}^{\mathrm d}$ have
complexity $15$, and both rules synchronize the lines of lengths $2,\dots,14$
(\cref{prop:nearmiss}).

The rules $\delta_{12}$ and $\delta_{13}$ have the same half-line on the cells
with $t+i\le78$, where they use the $22$ neighbourhoods
\begin{center}\small
\texttt{*GL>A GLL>G *AG>B AGL>L *BL>A BLG>L LGL>B *AL>G ALB>A LBG>L BGL>G}\\
\texttt{*GA>A GAL>L ALG>B LGG>G GGL>G BGG>L GGG>G ALL>A LLG>L ALA>A LAL>L}
\end{center}
besides $(\Lq,\Lq,\Lq)$; here \texttt{xyz>d} means $\delta(x,y,z)=d$. On these
cells the half-line has the pattern described before \cref{cor:regular}
(\cref{fig:halflines}). If this pattern continued forever, \cref{thm:left}
would exclude the half-line; \cref{lem:left} gives an explicit length without
that assumption.

\begin{corollary}[computer-assisted]\label{cor:germ12}
No five-state rule that has the $22$ transitions above synchronizes the line of
length $518$ in minimal time.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
With these transitions and $\delta(\Lq,\Lq,\Lq)=\Lq$, no other neighbourhood
occurs on the cells with $t+i\le1034$, so every such rule has the same diagram
$C_\infty$ there. On the anti-diagonal $t+i=1034$ the cells $2,\dots,259$ are in
state $\Aq$, and on $t+i=1033$ the cells $2,\dots,258$ are in state $\Lq$.
\Cref{lem:left} with $k=5$, $n=518$, $s=2$, $\ell=1$ and $Y=259$ would give
$257\le4^4=256$ (\cref{fig:delta12}).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Balzer's Conditions}\label{sec:balzer}

Balzer~\cite{Balzer67} reported that no five-state minimal-time solution
satisfies four extra conditions. In Mazoyer's account~\cite[\S7]{Mazoyer86L},
they are the stability of~$\Gq$, the condition of an image solution, that only
the neighbourhoods $(\Gq,\Gq,\Gq)$, $(\ast,\Gq,\Gq)$ and $(\Gq,\Gq,\ast)$ give
$\Fq$, and the transitions $(\Gq,v,\Gq)\mapsto\Gq$ for $v\ne\Gq$. We state them
as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[(B1)]
\item $\delta(x,\Gq,y)=\Gq$ unless $x,y\in\{\Gq,\ast\}$;
\item $\delta(I(z),I(y),I(x))=I(\delta(x,y,z))$ for every neighbourhood
$(x,y,z)$, including those with a border, where $I$ is an involution of the
states, extended by $I(\ast)=\ast$;
\item $\delta(\Gq,\Gq,\Gq)=\delta(\ast,\Gq,\Gq)=\delta(\Gq,\Gq,\ast)=\Fq$, and no
other neighbourhood gives $\Fq$;
\item $\delta(\Gq,v,\Gq)=\Gq$ for all $v\in W\setminus\{\Gq\}$.
\end{enumerate}
We call this the \emph{strong reading}. In the \emph{weak reading}, (B1) is
required only for working states $x,y$, and (B3) without its last clause.
Because (B2) is also required at the borders, the image of a computation is a
computation: if $c$ is a configuration of the line of length $n$ in working
states and $c'(i)=I(c(n+1-i))$ for $1\le i\le n$, then the successor of $c'$ is
the image of the successor of~$c$.
Yun\`es~\cite[\S2.7]{Yunes93} states (B1) and (B3) in the weak reading, but
(B2) only for the neighbourhoods without a border, and he admits states other
than~$\Gq$ in (B1), (B3) and (B4); we keep~$\Gq$, as in Mazoyer's account, and
return to the borders after \cref{prop:balzer}.

In both readings, $I$ fixes $\Fq$ and $\Gq$. Since $\delta$ is defined only on
neighbourhoods, which contain no~$\Fq$, (B2) can hold only if $I$ maps $W$ to
itself; hence $I(\Fq)=\Fq$. Then $I(\Gq)=\Gq$: otherwise
$g:=I(\Gq)\in W\setminus\{\Gq\}$, and (B2) and (B4) give
$\delta(g,\Gq,g)=I(\delta(\Gq,g,\Gq))=I(\Gq)=g$, whereas (B1) gives
$\delta(g,\Gq,g)=\Gq$. The conclusion is the same if, as in~\cite{Yunes93},
$\delta$ is defined on all triples of states and (B1), (B2) and (B4) are
required for all of them: the same argument excludes $g=\Fq$, and then (B2) and
(B3) give
$I(\Fq)=I(\delta(\Gq,\Gq,\Gq))=\delta(I(\Gq),I(\Gq),I(\Gq))=\delta(\Gq,\Gq,\Gq)=\Fq$.
So $I$ is one of the four involutions of $\{\Lq,\Aq,\Bq\}$, and (B1)--(B4)
become unit and binary clauses. As a check of this formalization, we evaluated
(B1)--(B4), extended to six states in the obvious way, on the transitions that
Mazoyer's six-state solution uses on the lines of lengths $3,\dots,150$ (an
exact finite simulation): (B1) in both readings, (B4), and (B2) for each of the
$10$ involutions of $\{\Lq,\Aq,\Bq,\Cq\}$, even without the borders, are
violated, and (B3) holds in the strong reading, in accordance with Mazoyer's
statement that his solution satisfies only the third
condition~\cite[\S7]{Mazoyer86L}.

\begin{proposition}[computer-assisted]\label{prop:balzer}
No five-state rule that satisfies (B1)--(B4) in the weak reading synchronizes
all lines of lengths $2,\dots,12$ in minimal time; a fortiori, no rule that
satisfies them in the strong reading does. The lengths up to $11$ do not
suffice to prove this.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For each involution, the clauses of (B1)--(B4) are added to
$\MT_5(\{2,\dots,N\};2N-2)$. For $I=(\Aq\,\Bq)$ and $N=10$, and for $I=(\Lq\,\Aq)$
and $I=(\Lq\,\Bq)$ and $N=9$, CaDiCaL refutes the formula with an LRAT proof.
For $I$ the identity and $N=12$, Kissat refutes it; \textsf{drat-trim} turns its
DRAT proof into an LRAT proof. Each of the four proofs is verified by
\texttt{lrat-check} and by \texttt{cake\_lpr}. The rule \texttt{balzer\_sym11} in \texttt{results/} of the
supplementary material satisfies
(B1)--(B4) in the strong reading with $I$ the identity and synchronizes every
line of length at most $11$ (direct simulation); see \cref{tab:balzer}.
\end{proof}

The condition (B2) at the borders is needed in \cref{prop:balzer}. The rule
\texttt{balzer\_noborder12} in \texttt{results/}, found by a solver, satisfies (B1)--(B4) in the weak reading with $I$ the
identity, except that (B2) holds only for the neighbourhoods without a border,
and synchronizes every line of length at most $12$ (direct simulation;
\cref{tab:balzer}). So with (B2) as Yun\`es states it,
the lines of lengths $2,\dots,12$ do not suffice, and we do not know whether
longer lines do.
