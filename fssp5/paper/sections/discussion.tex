\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

We summarise what the two barriers and the searches achieve, discuss why
five states seem to be much harder than six, and list open problems.

\paragraph{What the pumping lemma buys}
\Cref{lem:pumping} turns an infinite family of requirements on the
reflected triangles into constraints on the half-line diagram only. It is
satisfied by Mazoyer's solution, as it must be, and it eliminates, at
negligible cost, the whole class of rules whose half-line becomes periodic
behind the front too early. \Cref{thm:third} shows that the constraint has a
transparent geometric meaning: in every minimal-time solution the
non-periodic part of the half-line reaches the line $i=t/3$. For Mazoyer's
solution every depth-row $j<700$ is $3$-periodic from time $2j+1$ or $2j+2$,
i.e.\ the periodic zone behind the front is bounded by a signal of speed
$1/2$, comfortably within the bound.

\paragraph{Why lucky rules are hard to kill}
Small sets of necessary conditions are typically met by rules that are not
solutions; the question is how many lengths are needed before all such rules
disappear (if no solution exists, some finite set of lengths suffices, since
there are finitely many rules). For four states the answer is $9$ (or $8$ with a long
half-line). For five states the rule space is larger by roughly $40$
orders of magnitude ($5^{94}$ versus $4^{43}$ transition tables), and our
experiments show rules for all lengths up to $14$, and partial rules for
any single additional length we tried. In our experiments, the rules that
survive the pumping constraints are of two kinds. Either their half-lines
consist of finitely many periodic regions separated by signals, with a
boundary of speed $1/3$ (as for $\delta_{12}$ and $\delta_{13}$); such
half-lines are periodic in a wedge at the left border and are excluded by
\cref{thm:left}, although only for long lines. Or their half-lines are
chaotic (as for $\delta_{14}$); these pass both barriers in the ranges we
checked, and at present only completion tests on longer lines can exclude
them.

\paragraph{The mirrored construction}
The cone of \cref{lem:cone} ends near the point where the anti-diagonal
$t+i=2n-2$ meets the line $i=t/3$ (\cref{fig:anatomy}). By \cref{lem:determinacy}, the part of $R_n$ whose cone lies
in the periodic zone behind the front is the same for all large $n$ of a
given residue class, and the information that makes the right end fire
exactly at time $2n-2$ enters $R_n$ near that crossing and travels to the
right end at maximal speed. Heuristically, the right half of every
reflected triangle therefore has to behave like a \emph{mirrored}
minimal-time synchronization, started by the reflection and running on the
background left behind the front instead of on quiescent cells. A
five-state solution would have to realize both constructions---the direct
one on the quiescent background and the mirrored one on the front's
background---with the same four working states. We regard this tension as
the structural reason why five states are hard. In Balzer's image solutions
(\cref{sec:balzer}) the mirrored construction is the direct one with the
states renamed by an involution; together with his three other conditions
this is impossible with five states (\cref{prop:balzer}). \Cref{rem:right} turns one
half of the heuristic into a statement: seen from the right end, the
reflected triangles cannot be periodic over long stretches, so the mirrored
construction needs its own non-periodic structure near the right border. The
five rules for all lengths up to $14$ (\cref{sec:five} and \cref{app:more14}) fail at
$n=15$ in the same way, which is consistent with this picture: in each of
them the first cells to fire are in the right half of the line (cells $9$
to $15$), at times between $21$ and $27$ instead of $28$, and no cell near
the left border fires early.
A mirrored version of \cref{thm:third} is still missing (the mirrored
problem has no border at its far end), and turning the tension into an
impossibility proof is the most promising route to a conceptual answer.

\paragraph{The left half}
The same time budget applies on the left. The reflected wave reaches
cell~$i$ at time $2n-1-i$ and every cell fires at time $2n-2$; information
created behind the wave cannot overtake it, so the cells to the left of the
midpoint can only combine what the half-line has stored around them with
what a bounded number of chains immediately behind the wave bring from the
right. \Cref{thm:left} turns this into a theorem: the half-line cannot be
periodic in a wedge at the left border. In the classical constructions the
required structure is the hierarchy of slow signals whose crossings with
the wave mark the recursive division points; the common half-line of the
rules of \cref{thm:frontier} has none, and \cref{cor:germ12} excludes it.
The bound of \cref{lem:left} is exponential in $s$ and linear in the
period, so the lemma is weak for short lines; a sharper version, or a
version for half-lines that are only \emph{eventually} periodic along the
anti-diagonals, would strengthen the search of \cref{sec:five}
considerably.

\paragraph{Chaotic half-lines}
Half-lines that pass \cref{thm:third,thm:left} can be hierarchical, like
the half-line of Mazoyer's six-state solution, or chaotic. All five-state
half-lines that passed every test of \cref{sec:germs} and that we inspected
are chaotic, like the one of $\delta_{14}$, which is essentially the
elementary rule $22$. A germ of complexity $c$ leaves $94-c$ transitions
free; for the germ of $\delta_{14}$ these are $78$, i.e.\
$5^{78}\approx2^{181}$ completions. Each additional length $n$ imposes of the
order of $n$ independent-looking conditions on the reflected triangle. If
these conditions behaved like independent events of probability $1/2$,
about $2^{181-N^2/2}$ completions would survive the lengths up to $N$, so
the completions of a chaotic germ would run out around $N\approx20$; the
rule $\delta_{14}$ and the completion runs of \cref{sec:germs} are
consistent with this heuristic, which we have not been able to turn into a
proof. For the left border the difficulty is concrete: by the proof of
\cref{lem:left}, the firing of the cell $2s$ depends only on the input
anti-diagonals at the cells $i\ge s$ and on a band of $2s$ chains; in a
chaotic medium without markers near the border, such a band has no evident
way of recognising the cell $2s$.

\paragraph{Open problems}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item Does a five-state minimal-time solution exist? By
  \cref{prop:chaotic}, a negative answer along the lines of \cref{thm:four}
  requires lines of length at least $15$.
\item Is there a necessary condition, in the spirit of \cref{lem:pumping},
  that constrains the reflected triangles themselves and not only the
  half-line?
\item What is the smallest $N$ such that some five-state rule is
  $\{2,\dots,N\}$-minimal but no five-state rule is
  $\{2,\dots,N+1\}$-minimal, if such an $N$ exists? We know $N\ge14$.
\item Can the bound $(k-1)^{2s}L$ of \cref{lem:left} be replaced by one
  that is polynomial in $s$, or by one that also covers anti-diagonals that
  are periodic only up to a bounded number of defects? Either would make the
  lemma effective on shorter lines and on half-lines whose periodic regions
  start far from the border (\cref{app:k16}).
\end{enumerate}
