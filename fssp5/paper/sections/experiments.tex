\section{Four states: a certified proof}\label{sec:four}

All formulas are produced by the generator \texttt{gencnf.py}; models are
decoded into rule tables and re-simulated by the independent brute-force
checker \texttt{fsspcheck.c}. The programs were written with the help of a
generative AI system (see the declaration before the references). Solvers: Kissat~4.0.4 \cite{Kissat20} and
CaDiCaL~3.0.1 \cite{CaDiCaL2} (or CaDiCaL~1.9.5 through PySAT
\cite{PySAT18} for incremental runs). Times are single-core wall-clock times
on a cloud virtual CPU.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:four}
No rule with four states $\{\mathsf L,\mathsf G,\mathsf A,\mathsf F\}$ satisfying
\eqref{eq:quiescence} synchronizes all lines of lengths $2,\dots,9$ in
minimal time. In particular there is no four-state minimal-time solution,
even without the condition $\delta(\ast,\mathsf L,\mathsf L)=\mathsf L$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every such rule yields a model of $\mathrm{MT}_4(\{2,\dots,9\};16)$ (for
$k=4$ there is a single auxiliary state and no symmetry breaking). Indeed, a
half-line cell $(t,i)$ with $t+i\le16$ is quiescent if $i\ge10$
(\cref{lem:lightcone}) and coincides with the cell $(t,i)$ of the line of
length $9$, at a time $t<16$, if $i\le9$ (\cref{lem:agree}), so none of
them fires; the lines are synchronized by assumption; and the return-chain
constraints hold by \cref{lem:return}, applied to the lengths $n$ and $n+1$
for $2\le n\le8$. CaDiCaL proves this
formula unsatisfiable in $51$\,s and emits an LRAT proof
\cite{CruzFilipeEtAl17} of $177$\,MB, which \texttt{lrat-check} of the \textsf{drat-trim}
suite \cite{WetzlerHeuleHunt14} verifies in $5.7$\,s. The SHA-256 digests of
the formula and of the proof are listed in \ref{app:repro}.
\end{proof}

The bound $9$ is sharp for the plain formula: $\mathrm{MT}_4(\{2,\dots,8\};14)$
is satisfiable, and an independent depth-first enumeration (program
\texttt{fsspdfs.c}, which fills the table lazily in the order in which
transitions are needed) finds the numbers of partial rules listed in
\cref{tab:four}, counted on the transitions they use; it reaches zero exactly
at the lengths $2,\dots,9$, in agreement with the solver. These numbers
agree with the earlier searches. Yun\`es \cite[\S3.1]{Yunes93}, repeating
Balzer's search with the additional condition $\delta(\ast,\mathsf
L,\mathsf L)=\mathsf L$, found exactly $27$ four-state automata (counted on
the transitions they use) that synchronize the lengths $2,\dots,8$, and none
that also synchronizes the length $9$; none of our $27$ partial rules uses
the neighbourhood $(\ast,\mathsf L,\mathsf L)$. Sanders, who imposes only
\eqref{eq:quiescence}, shows a four-state rule that works up to length $8$
\cite[Fig.~2]{Sanders94}; it is one of our $27$ partial rules. An
independent recent search \cite{AverageUser21} reports the same numbers.
What \cref{thm:four} adds is the certificate: the earlier proofs rest on
the correctness of search programs, and Sanders himself left open
``whether one accepts the output of a C program as a proof''
\cite{Sanders94}. With the half-line extended to
anti-diagonal $78$ the lengths $2,\dots,8$ already suffice. The pumping
constraints alone are weak for four states: together with the half-line up
to anti-diagonal~$78$, the lengths $2,\dots,7$ remain satisfiable.

\section{Five states: the frontier}\label{sec:five}

For five states we can neither exhibit a solution nor prove that none
exists; this section measures how far the tools of the previous sections
reach. \Cref{sec:lucky} shows what a solver returns when it is asked for a
rule that works for a small set of lengths. \Cref{sec:fourteen} presents
explicit rules that synchronize every line of length at most $14$, the
current frontier, and shows how the barriers of \cref{sec:pumping} exclude
the half-lines of some of them. \Cref{sec:germs} turns the question around:
instead of searching for rules, it enumerates the possible half-lines of
low complexity and excludes all but a few of them. \Cref{sec:balzer}
checks Balzer's conditional five-state result, and \cref{sec:difficulty}
estimates the size of the remaining search.

\subsection{Lucky rules}\label{sec:lucky}

For five states the table has $94$ free transitions with five values each,
against $43$ with four values for four states (in both cases the neighbourhoods
$(\ast,y,\ast)$ and the two quiescence transitions are excluded): the search
space grows by a factor of about $5^{94}/4^{43}\approx10^{40}$.
\Cref{tab:five} lists representative instances. All of them are
satisfiable, and in each case the decoded rule fails at the first length
that was not encoded: a solver asked for a rule that works for a finite set
of lengths has no reason to find one that works for more. We call such
rules \emph{lucky}. Two kinds occur. Rules of the first kind have a \emph{periodic} half-line behind the
front (\cref{fig:diagrams}(b)); they can even synchronize an isolated long
line such as $n=20$, but they violate the pumping lemma massively (155 of the
pairs $4\le n<n'\le40$ for the rule shown). Rules of the second kind have a
chaotic half-line which, without the half-line constraints, typically fires
at some point. Once the half-line is extended and the pumping constraints
are imposed, the half-lines produced by the solver change character. In
the models we examined in detail, a region delimited by a line of speed
$1/3$ appears, as predicted by \cref{thm:third}: see
\cref{fig:diagrams}(c) and the rules of \cref{thm:frontier}, whose common
half-line consists of a front of $\mathsf G$'s, a solid wake of
$\mathsf G$'s and a region $\mathsf{A}\,\mathsf{L}\,\mathsf{A}\,\mathsf{L}\cdots$
behind a boundary of speed exactly $1/3$. Chaotic half-lines
that never fire satisfy the pumping constraints as well; one of them is
discussed after \cref{cor:germ12}.

\subsection{Five-state rules for all lengths up to fourteen}\label{sec:fourteen}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:frontier}
The five-state rules $\delta_{12}$ and $\delta_{13}$ of
\cref{tab:delta12,tab:delta13} have the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\delta_{12}$ synchronizes the line of length $n$ at time $2n-2$ for
  every $2\le n\le 12$, and one step before firing all cells are in state
  $\mathsf A$. It does not synchronize the line of length $13$: a cell fires
  at time $23$.
\item $\delta_{13}$ synchronizes the line of length $n$ at time $2n-2$ for
  every $2\le n\le 13$, and one step before firing all cells are in state
  $\mathsf A$ whenever $4\le n\le 13$. It does not synchronize the line of
  length $14$: a cell fires at time $21$.
\item The half-line diagrams of $\delta_{12}$ and $\delta_{13}$ coincide on
  all cells $(t,i)$ with $t+i\le 78$; these cells use $22$ neighbourhoods
  besides $(\mathsf L,\mathsf L,\mathsf L)$, on which the two rules agree, and
  none of them is a firing cell.
\item Both rules satisfy $\mathrm{PUMP}(n,n')$ for all $4\le n<n'\le 40$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each property is a finite computation on the explicit tables, carried out
by direct simulation: (a), (b) and the failures at $n=13$ and $n=14$ with
the checker \texttt{fsspcheck.c}, (c) with \texttt{check\_halfline.py}
and \texttt{germcompare.py}, and (d) with \texttt{pumping.py}, which recomputes the cones of
\cref{lem:cone} by breadth-first search (\ref{app:repro}).
\end{proof}

The rule $\delta_{12}$ was found by Kissat on
$\mathrm{MT}_5(\{2,\dots,12\};78,40)$ with links and the uniform pre-firing
constraint (\cref{tab:five}); $\delta_{13}$ was found by the same solver
after fixing the $22$ half-line neighbourhoods of $\delta_{12}$ and adding
the length~$13$, in $108$\,s. The two rules differ in $57$ of the remaining
entries.

Consequently, the necessary conditions used in this paper,
restricted to lengths at most $13$ (even together with the pumping
constraints up to $40$ and the half-line up to anti-diagonal $78$), are
satisfiable (\cref{prop:chaotic} below extends this to length $14$), and
the requirement ``uniform configuration before firing'', satisfied by
Mazoyer's solution, does not help for the lengths up to $12$.

The half-line shared by the two rules
consists of a front of $\mathsf G$'s, a solid wake of $\mathsf G$'s and,
behind a boundary of speed exactly $1/3$, a region
$\mathsf{A}\,\mathsf{L}\,\mathsf{A}\,\mathsf{L}\cdots$ that is periodic in
time and space. By the left-border barrier this architecture cannot be
completed to a solution:

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:germ12}
No rule that agrees with $\delta_{12}$ on the $22$ neighbourhoods of
\cref{thm:frontier}(c) synchronizes the line of length $518$ in minimal
time. In particular no minimal-time solution has the half-line of
$\delta_{12}$ and $\delta_{13}$ below anti-diagonal~$78$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Simulating the half-line with these $22$ neighbourhoods (and
$(\mathsf L,\mathsf L,\mathsf L)$) only, no other neighbourhood occurs on
the cells with $t+i\le1034$; hence every such rule has the same diagram
$C_\infty$ there. On the anti-diagonal $t+i=1034$ the cells $2,\dots,259$
are all in state $\mathsf A$, and on $t+i=1033$ the cells $2,\dots,258$ are
all in state $\mathsf L$. \Cref{lem:left} with $k=5$, $n=518$, $s=2$,
$L=1$ and $Y=259$ would give $257\le4^4=256$.
\end{proof}

So no choice of the remaining transitions can rescue this architecture.
The lemma only bites at length $518$, whereas the two rules themselves
already fail at lengths $13$ and $14$. The regularity certificate of
\cref{lem:certificate} (with $P=6$) gives a second proof of the last statement
of \cref{cor:germ12} that does not use a particular length.

The frontier is not tied to this architecture, and it is not at
thirteen. The classification of \cref{sec:germs} below left a few germs of
complexity $16$ undecided. For one of them, Kissat completes the germ to a
rule for the lengths $2,\dots,13$ in $442$\,s (file
\texttt{delta13\_chaotic.txt}); fixing the same germ and asking for the
lengths $2,\dots,14$, together with the links and the pumping constraints
of \cref{sec:encoding}, it finds in $1758$\,s a rule for all lengths up to
fourteen, with a half-line of an entirely different kind
(\cref{fig:halflines}).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:chaotic}
The five-state rule $\delta_{14}$ of \cref{tab:delta14} synchronizes the
line of length $n$ at time $2n-2$ for every $2\le n\le14$ and fails for
$n=15$ (a cell fires at time $22$). Its half-line diagram uses $16$
neighbourhoods besides $(\mathsf L,\mathsf L,\mathsf L)$ on all cells with
$t+i\le10^4$, has no firing cell there, and satisfies $\mathrm{PUMP}(n,n')$
for all $4\le n<n'\le800$; for $n\le5000$, $s\le3$ and $L\le24$ its input
anti-diagonals have no stretch to which \cref{lem:left} applies.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Direct simulation with the programs \texttt{fsspcheck.c},
\texttt{germcompare.py}, \texttt{germext.c} and \texttt{pumping.py}.
\end{proof}

The half-line of $\delta_{14}$ (\cref{fig:halflines}(b)) is chaotic. For
$3\le t<1500$, cell~$1$ takes only the values
$\mathsf L,\mathsf G$, cell~$2$ only $\mathsf L,\mathsf A$, and all other
cells only $\mathsf L,\mathsf B$; on the latter the germ is the elementary
cellular automaton \emph{rule $22$} in the numbering of \cite{Wolfram83}
(a cell becomes $\mathsf B$ if and only if exactly one cell of its
neighbourhood is $\mathsf B$), a classical example of chaotic behaviour,
driven at its left end by the two border columns.

The reflected triangles of $\delta_{14}$ end with a recognisable
wave: for $4\le n\le14$ the configuration one step before firing is
$\mathsf L\,\mathsf G^{\,n-2}\,\mathsf B$, and the state $\mathsf G$, which
the half-line uses only in its first column, marks the firing wave against
the chaotic background. This mechanism does not extend: with the germ of
$\delta_{14}$ fixed, no rule synchronizes all lengths $2,\dots,15$ with
these configurations before firing (Kissat, unsatisfiable after about
$30$ minutes); without this restriction the lengths $2,\dots,15$ remain
undecided after several hours.

The half-line has non-periodic structure everywhere, and in the ranges of \cref{prop:chaotic} neither barrier of
\cref{sec:pumping} excludes it; the only arguments that we can apply to it
are completion tests for longer lines, which are expensive. Consequently, the
necessary conditions used in this paper, restricted to lengths at most $14$
(together with the pumping constraints up to $40$ and the half-line up to
anti-diagonal $78$), are satisfiable, and a refutation of five-state
minimal-time solutions along these lines must involve lines of length at
least $15$.

\Cref{fig:squads14} shows $\delta_{14}$ at work on the lines of lengths
$14$ and $15$. Four further rules synchronize every line of length at most
$14$, on other germs (\cref{app:more14}): $\delta_{14}^{\mathrm b}$, whose
half-line is eventually regular, so that no completion of its germ is a
solution (\cref{lem:certificate}); $\delta_{14}^{\mathrm c}$ and
$\delta_{14}^{\mathrm d}$, on the two surviving germs of complexity $15$,
which share one half-line up to a renaming of the states; and
$\delta_{14}^{\mathrm e}$, on a germ of complexity $17$. All five fail at
$n=15$ in the same way: cells in the right half of the line fire one to
seven steps early (\cref{tab:more14}).

\subsection{Half-lines of low complexity}\label{sec:germs}

The rules of \cref{sec:fourteen} suggest a change of viewpoint. The
half-line conditions (no cell of the half-line fires, the front is never in
state $\mathsf L$, and the pumping constraints) involve only the
transitions that the half-line actually uses, and a half-line that uses few
distinct neighbourhoods is determined by few transitions. Instead of
searching for complete rules, we can therefore enumerate all half-lines of
low complexity and try to exclude each of them, first by a quick
completion test and then by the barriers of \cref{sec:pumping}.

For a rule $\delta$ and a bound $M$, the \emph{germ} of $\delta$ below $M$
is the restriction of $\delta$ to the neighbourhoods
$(C_\infty(t-1,i-1),C_\infty(t-1,i),C_\infty(t-1,i+1))$ of the cells
$(t,i)$ with $t\ge1$ and $t+i\le M$ of its half-line diagram, and its
\emph{complexity} $c_M(\delta)$ is the number of these neighbourhoods other
than $(\mathsf L,\mathsf L,\mathsf L)$. The germ determines $C_\infty$ on
all cells with $t+i\le M$. The rules of \cref{thm:frontier} have $c_{78}=22$
and Mazoyer's rule has $c_{78}=57$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:germs}
Every five-state minimal-time solution $\delta$ satisfies $c_{78}(\delta)\ge15$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof (computer-assisted)]
Let $\delta$ be a five-state minimal-time solution with $c_{78}(\delta)\le14$.
Order the cells of $C_\infty$ by anti-diagonal and, within an anti-diagonal,
by increasing time; after exchanging the names $\mathsf A$ and $\mathsf B$
if necessary, $\mathsf A$ occurs before $\mathsf B$ in this order (if at
all). By \eqref{eq:neverfire}, \cref{lem:return,lem:pumping}, the germ of
$\delta$ below $78$ produces no firing cell, never produces $\mathsf L$ at
the front, and satisfies $\mathrm{PUMP}(n,n')$ for $4\le n<n'\le40$.

\emph{Enumeration.} The program \texttt{germdfs.c} enumerates all germs
with these properties, with at most $14$ neighbourhoods besides
$(\mathsf L,\mathsf L,\mathsf L)$ and with $\mathsf A$ before $\mathsf B$,
by a depth-first search over the cells in the above order in which the
value of a neighbourhood is chosen when it first occurs: $1.0\cdot10^8$
nodes, $244$ germs. An independent re-implementation in Python
(\texttt{germdfs\_check.py}), which recomputes the cones of \cref{lem:cone}
by breadth-first search, finds the same $244$ germs.

\emph{Completion test.} For $230$ of them the formula $\mathrm{MT}_5(\{2,\dots,10\};78,39)$ (without symmetry breaking,
which the enumeration has already performed) together with the germ is
unsatisfiable; a single formula in which a selector variable chooses the
germ is refuted by CaDiCaL in $4$\,s, with an LRAT proof ($11$\,MB) checked
by \texttt{lrat-check} in under a second.

\emph{Half-line argument.} Each of the remaining $14$ germs determines $C_\infty$ far beyond anti-diagonal $78$ (no new neighbourhood
occurs up to anti-diagonal $3300$), and on these extended diagrams
\cref{lem:left} applies with $s=1$: for each germ there are $n\le163$ and
$L\le5$ such that the two input anti-diagonals of the line of length $n$ are
$L$-periodic on $[1,Y]$ with $Y-1>16L$ (for instance $n=28$, $L=1$, $Y=19$).
An independent simulation (\texttt{germ\_k14\_check.py}) confirms both facts
for each of the $14$ germs, below anti-diagonal $330$.
Hence no rule containing one of the $244$ germs is a minimal-time solution.
\end{proof}

\Cref{tab:germs} shows how the two kinds of arguments share the work when
the same pipeline runs on the $9{,}787$ germs of complexity at most $16$
(details in \cref{app:k16}). The completion test for the lengths
$2,\dots,10$, certified by one LRAT proof, refutes $8{,}824$ of them;
\cref{lem:left,lem:pumping}, applied to the half-lines extended up to
anti-diagonal $10^4$, refute $931$ of the survivors; a completion test for
the lengths $2,\dots,13$ and the regularity certificate of
\cref{lem:certificate} refute $8$ more each. The half-line arguments are
also strong on their own: without any SAT call they refute at least
$8{,}263$ of the $9{,}787$ germs.

The $16$ germs that remain, among them the germ of $\delta_{14}$, are irregular
as far as our certificates can tell, and those we inspected are chaotic.
Two of them have complexity exactly $15$ and share one half-line up to a
renaming of the states; both extend to rules for all lengths up to $14$
($\delta_{14}^{\mathrm c}$ and $\delta_{14}^{\mathrm d}$). Raising the bound
of \cref{prop:germs} to $16$ therefore amounts to refuting this half-line,
which requires lines of length at least $15$; a cube-and-conquer attack on
the lengths $2,\dots,15$ refuted thousands of cubes but left the question
undecided (\cref{app:cube}). For the $43{,}294$ germs of complexity exactly
$17$ the same filters leave $187$, again with an LRAT certificate for the
completion test, and one of the survivors yields $\delta_{14}^{\mathrm e}$
(\cref{app:k17}).

\subsection{Balzer's conditions}\label{sec:balzer}

Balzer \cite[p.~37]{Balzer67} also reported that no five-state minimal-time
solution satisfies four additional conditions, ``the weakest conditions we
found which enabled the program to prove that no five state minimal time
solution existed''. In our notation, and numbered as in Mazoyer's account
\cite[\S7]{Mazoyer86Lyon} (Balzer lists (B2) first and (B1) second), they
read:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(B\arabic*)]
\item $\mathsf G$ is stable (Balzer: a ``resident state''):
  $\delta(x,\mathsf G,y)=\mathsf G$ unless both $x$ and $y$ belong to
  $\{\mathsf G,\ast\}$;
\item the rule is an image solution (\cref{app:gl-squad}) for some
  involution $I$ \cite[p.~32]{Balzer67};
\item $\delta(\mathsf G,\mathsf G,\mathsf G)=\delta(\ast,\mathsf G,\mathsf
  G)=\delta(\mathsf G,\mathsf G,\ast)=\mathsf F$, and no other
  neighbourhood is mapped to $\mathsf F$ (Balzer: $\mathsf G$ is the
  ``get-ready-to-fire'' state);
\item $\delta(\mathsf G,v,\mathsf G)=\mathsf G$ for every working state
  $v\neq\mathsf G$.
\end{enumerate}
Balzer places the general at the right end of the line; all four conditions
are invariant under reflection. We call (B1)--(B4), which is Balzer's
wording, the \emph{strong reading}. Yun\`es \cite[\S2.7]{Yunes93} restates
the conditions for arbitrary states and in a weaker form: (B1) only for
working states $x,y$, and (B3) without the requirement that no other
neighbourhood produces $\mathsf F$. We call (B1)--(B4) with these two
weakenings, still for the state $\mathsf G$, the \emph{weak reading}.
Balzer adds that his program could not prove the same for six states, and
that his eight-state solution satisfies all four conditions. Mazoyer's six-state
solution satisfies only (B3) \cite{Mazoyer86Lyon}, and our formalization
gives the same verdict: on the
$117$ transitions that it uses on the lines of lengths $3,\dots,150$, (B1)
and (B4) fail, (B2) fails for every involution, and (B3) holds in the strong
reading (program \texttt{balzer\_mazoyer.py}). Sanders \cite{Sanders94}
notes that the error in Balzer's search ``made this approach look better
than it is''.

All four conditions fix transitions or relate pairs of transitions, so they
are added to $\mathrm{MT}_5(\{2,\dots,N\})$ as unit and binary clauses (program
\texttt{balzer.py}, without symmetry breaking). We take $I(\mathsf
F)=\mathsf F$; then (B3) forces $I(\mathsf G)=\mathsf G$, so $I$ is one of
the four involutions of $\{\mathsf L,\mathsf A,\mathsf B\}$, and we treat
them separately. In the weak reading, no rule with $I=(\mathsf A\,\mathsf
B)$ synchronizes the lengths $2,\dots,10$ and no rule with $I=(\mathsf
L\,\mathsf A)$ or $I=(\mathsf L\,\mathsf B)$ the lengths $2,\dots,9$
(CaDiCaL, $85$\,s, $37$\,s and $37$\,s; LRAT certificates checked by
\texttt{lrat-check} in $7.1$\,s, $3.5$\,s and $3.3$\,s). The symmetric rules ($I$ the
identity) go further: in both readings there are rules satisfying
(B1)--(B4) that synchronize every line of length at most $11$ (file
\path{results/balzer_sym11.txt}), but even in the weak reading no such
rule synchronizes the lengths $2,\dots,12$ (Kissat, $16$ minutes; DRAT proof
of $958$\,MB verified by \textsf{drat-trim} \cite{WetzlerHeuleHunt14} in
$31$ minutes; for the strong reading CaDiCaL needs $119$\,s and gives an
LRAT certificate of $477$\,MB, checked in $8.5$\,s). Since the strong
reading only adds conditions, this gives:

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:balzer}
No five-state rule satisfying (B1)--(B4) in the weak reading, with an
involution $I$ that fixes $\mathsf F$, synchronizes all lines of lengths
$2,\dots,12$ in minimal time; a fortiori none satisfying the strong reading
does. In particular Balzer's conditional result holds, even for the weaker
form of the conditions, and the lengths up to $11$ do not suffice to prove
it.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Where the difficulty lies}\label{sec:difficulty}

Once a germ is fixed, the half-line is determined up to anti-diagonal $78$
and only the reflected triangles remain. For three lucky rules of
\cref{tab:five}, those for $\{2,\dots,9,16\}$, for $\{2,\dots,9,20\}$ and,
found with the half-line up to anti-diagonal $78$, for $2,\dots,10$, Kissat
decides the completion problem of the germ in less than one second: the
first two germs have no completion even for the lengths $2,\dots,8$, the
third has completions for the lengths up to $10$ but not up to $11$. (The
half-line of the rule for $2,\dots,12$, found with the half-line only up to
anti-diagonal $22$, fires below anti-diagonal $78$.) Fixing the $58$ transitions that
Mazoyer's rule uses on its half-line up to anti-diagonal $78$ leaves a
completion problem for the lengths $3,\dots,12$ (without symmetry breaking)
that Kissat solves in $4$\,s. The chaotic germs are the exception: the
completions of the germ of $\delta_{14}$ for the lengths up to $12$, $13$
and $14$ take $2$\,s, $442$\,s and $1758$\,s (the last one with links), and
for the lengths up to $15$ a Kissat run without links stopped without an
answer after four hours.

To measure the size of the whole search, we estimated the natural
look-ahead search tree---half-line transitions fixed in order of first use,
anti-diagonal by anti-diagonal, a node being discarded when the full
formula with the partial table as assumptions is refuted within $3000$
conflicts---with Knuth's random-probe estimator \cite{Knuth75}: a probe
follows a random path from the root to a leaf, and if $d_1,\dots,d_D$ are
the numbers of children of the nodes of the path before the leaf, then
$1+d_1+d_1d_2+\dots+d_1\cdots d_D$ is an unbiased estimate of the number of
nodes. For four states ($\mathrm{MT}_4(\{2,\dots,9\};16,8)$ with links) the
mean of this estimate over $30$ probes is $25.5$ nodes. For five states
($\mathrm{MT}_5(\{2,\dots,12\};78,39)$ with links) three probes reach depths
$30$ to $36$; their last terms $d_1\cdots d_D$, which are lower bounds for
their estimates, are $4.2\cdot10^{11}$, $3.1\cdot10^{17}$ and
$8.3\cdot10^{18}$, and the mean estimate is $6.7\cdot10^{18}$ nodes. Adding
the lengths up to $18$ changes little (last terms $2.1\cdot10^{11}$,
$5.1\cdot10^{16}$ and $4.2\cdot10^{18}$, mean estimate $2.2\cdot10^{18}$).
The estimator has a large variance and three probes are few, but every
five-state probe exceeds the four-state estimate by at least nine orders
of magnitude, in line with the fact that
our runs on the unrestricted five-state formulas with the pumping
constraints for the lengths $2,\dots,N$ with $N=12$, $14$ and $18$ (up to
about $1.5$ CPU-hours each) did not terminate. Clause learning compresses
such trees considerably, and cube-and-conquer \cite{HeuleEtAl11}
parallelizes them well, but a complete five-state search along these lines
is a computation of a much larger scale than the ones reported here.
Earlier attempts give the same picture. Balzer stopped his five-state
search after three hours and about $570{,}000$ partial rules
\cite[p.~36]{Balzer67}, Yun\`es \cite[\S3.1]{Yunes93} could not complete
it either, and Sanders \cite{Sanders94},
whose program proves the four-state bound in less than $11$ seconds on a
MasPar with $16384$ processors, estimated that a five-state search would
take about $10^{16}$ times the age of the universe on that machine; a
$24$-hour randomized search for a five-state solution found nothing.
