\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The firing squad synchronization problem (FSSP), posed by Myhill in 1957
and popularised by Moore~\cite{Moore64}, asks for a finite automaton that,
placed in every cell of a line of arbitrary length $n$ and started with all
cells quiescent except a \emph{general} at one end, drives all cells into a
common \emph{firing} state for the first time at the same instant
(\cref{fig:squad}). The difficulty is that the cells are identical, that
each of them only sees its two neighbours, and that none of them can count
up to $n$: with $k$ states a cell can tell only $k$ situations apart, yet
the same rule has to work for every length.

Goto \cite{Goto62} and Waksman \cite{Waksman66} showed that the problem can
be solved in time $2n-2$, which is optimal (\cref{rem:lowerbound}). Since then the
minimum number of states of a \emph{minimal-time} solution has been a
benchmark question in cellular automata theory \cite{Mazoyer86,Umeo09}:
Waksman used $16$ states, Balzer $8$ \cite{Balzer67}, Gerken $7$
\cite{Gerken87} and Mazoyer $6$ \cite{Mazoyer87}. On the other side, Balzer
\cite{Balzer67} reported, and Sanders \cite{Sanders94} confirmed with a
corrected exhaustive search, that no $4$-state minimal-time solution exists
(\cref{fig:scoreboard}). Whether five states suffice has remained open since
Mazoyer's construction; the question is recorded as open in the surveys
\cite{Umeo09,Correa17}. Many six-state minimal-time solutions are known
today \cite{Clergue18,NguyenMaignan20}; solutions with at most five states
are known for restricted families of lengths, for instance the powers of
two, some of them in non-minimal time \cite{UmeoYanagihara07,Yunes08}, and
four-state symmetric protocols synchronize the rings whose lengths are
powers of two \cite{UmeoKamikawaYunes09}. But no five-state minimal-time
solution has been found, nor has its existence been refuted.

\paragraph{This paper}
We do not settle the five-state question. We develop tools that apply to
minimal-time solutions with any number of states, use them to give a
certified proof of the four-state lower bound that is produced and checked
within a minute, and measure precisely how far the same tools reach for
five states.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{Agreement and reflection} (\cref{sec:prelim}). The line of
  length $n$ coincides with the half-line below the anti-diagonal
  $t+i=2n-2$; hence all lines are described by a single half-line diagram
  $C_\infty$ plus one \emph{reflected triangle} $R_n$ per length, and $R_n$ is
  a function of two anti-diagonals of $C_\infty$ only
  (\cref{lem:agree,lem:determinacy}).
\item \emph{Two barriers} (\cref{sec:pumping}). In every minimal-time
  solution the input words of the reflected triangles of any two lengths
  $4\le n<n'$ differ on an explicit cone of about $n/2$ cells
  (\cref{lem:pumping}), a constraint on the half-line alone. It implies that
  the non-periodic part of the half-line reaches the line $i=t/3$
  (\cref{thm:third}), which explains a priori the speed-$1/3$ signal of the
  classical constructions. At the other end, the first chains of the
  reflected triangle form a finite-state system that must fire the cells near
  the left border exactly when it reaches them; hence the input
  anti-diagonals cannot be periodic over long stretches near the left border
  (\cref{lem:left}), the half-line cannot be periodic in any wedge at the
  border (\cref{thm:left}), and in particular it is never eventually regular
  (\cref{cor:regular}).
\item \emph{A validated encoding and a certified four-state bound}
  (\cref{sec:encoding,sec:four}). We encode the bounded problem, together
  with the consequences above, into propositional logic, validate the
  encoding against Mazoyer's solution extracted mechanically from Duprat's
  Coq formalization \cite{Duprat97}, and obtain an LRAT certificate, checked
  in $6$ seconds, that no four-state minimal-time solution exists
  (\cref{thm:four}), even without the left quiescence condition.
\item \emph{The five-state frontier} (\cref{sec:five}). Explicit five-state
  rules satisfy all our half-line conditions and synchronize every line of
  length at most $13$ (\cref{thm:frontier}), but their common half-line is
  excluded by \cref{lem:left} (\cref{cor:germ12}). A rule with a chaotic
  half-line, $\delta_{14}$, synchronizes every line of length at most $14$
  (\cref{prop:chaotic}), and so do four further rules; so every refutation
  along these lines must use lines of length at least~$15$. Combining both
  lemmas with certified solver calls, every five-state solution uses at
  least $15$ distinct neighbourhoods on its half-line below anti-diagonal
  $78$ (\cref{prop:germs}); of the $9{,}787$ half-lines with at most $16$
  neighbourhoods, only $16$ irregular ones survive.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Related work}
Surveys of the FSSP are \cite{Mazoyer86,Umeo09}; a classical textbook
exposition is \cite{Minsky67}. Mazoyer \cite{Mazoyer96} studied the
structure of optimal solutions and the information flow they require;
Settle and Simon \cite{SettleSimon02} obtained small non-minimal-time
solutions. Beyond Balzer and Sanders, lower bounds on the number of states
are known for variants: no three-state solution and no four-state symmetric
minimal-time solution exist for rings \cite{BerthiaumeEtAl04}; for further
restricted classes see \cite{Correa17}. Computer searches have been used to
find new solutions \cite{Clergue18} and to generate families of solutions
by local simulations \cite{NguyenMaignan20}. SAT solvers have been applied
to many extremal problems in combinatorics with certified unsatisfiability
proofs \cite{HeuleKullmannMarek16,Heule18}; we are not aware of previous
SAT-based work on the state complexity of the FSSP.

\paragraph{Organisation}
\Cref{sec:prelim} states the problem and reduces all lines to one
half-line and a family of reflected triangles. \Cref{sec:pumping} proves
the two barriers. \Cref{sec:encoding} gives the propositional encoding,
\cref{sec:four} the certified four-state bound and \cref{sec:five} the
five-state frontier; \cref{sec:discussion} discusses what separates five
states from six and lists open problems. \Cref{app:glossary} is a glossary
of all terms and symbols, \cref{app:further} records the further
experiments, \cref{app:delta12} gives the rule tables and \cref{app:repro}
explains how to reproduce every computation.
