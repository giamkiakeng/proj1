\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The firing squad synchronization problem (FSSP), posed by Myhill in 1957
and popularised by Moore~\cite{Moore64}, asks for a finite automaton that,
placed in every cell of a line of arbitrary length $n$ and started with all
cells quiescent except a \emph{general} at one end, drives all cells into a
common \emph{firing} state for the first time at the same instant. Goto
\cite{Goto62} and Waksman \cite{Waksman66} showed that this is possible in
time $2n-2$, which is optimal \cite{Moore64}. Since then the minimum number
of states of a \emph{minimal-time} solution has been a benchmark question
in cellular automata theory \cite{Mazoyer86,Umeo09}: Waksman used $16$
states, Balzer $8$ \cite{Balzer67}, Gerken $7$ \cite{Gerken87} and Mazoyer
$6$ \cite{Mazoyer87}. On the other side, Balzer \cite{Balzer67} reported,
and Sanders \cite{Sanders94} confirmed with a corrected exhaustive search,
that no $4$-state minimal-time solution exists. Whether five states suffice
has remained open since Mazoyer's construction; it is regarded as the
central open question on state complexity of the FSSP
\cite{Umeo09,Correa17}. Many six-state minimal-time solutions are known
today \cite{Clergue18,NguyenMaignan20}, and solutions with at most five
states are known for restricted families of lengths, for instance $n=2^k$,
some of them in non-minimal time
\cite{UmeoYanagihara07,Yunes08,UmeoKamikawaYunes09}, but no five-state
minimal-time solution has been found, nor has its existence been refuted.

\paragraph{This paper}
We do not settle the five-state question. We develop tools that apply to
minimal-time solutions with any number of states, use them to give a
certified proof of the four-state lower bound that is produced and checked
within a minute, and measure
precisely how far the same tools reach for five states.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{Agreement and reflection} (\cref{sec:prelim}). The line of
  length $n$ coincides with the half-line below the anti-diagonal
  $t+i=2n-2$; hence all lines are described by a single half-line diagram
  $C_\infty$ plus one \emph{reflected triangle} $R_n$ per length, and $R_n$ is
  a function of two anti-diagonals of $C_\infty$ only
  (\cref{lem:agree,lem:determinacy}).
\item \emph{Two barriers} (\cref{sec:pumping}). In every minimal-time
  solution the input words of the reflected triangles of any two lengths
  $4\le n<n'$ differ on an explicit cone of about $n/2$ cells
  (\cref{lem:pumping}). This is a constraint on the half-line alone, valid
  for \emph{all} pairs of lengths. It implies that the non-periodic part of
  the half-line always reaches the line $i=t/3$ (\cref{thm:third}), an a
  priori explanation of the speed-$1/3$ signal present in the classical
  constructions. At the other end, the first chains of the reflected
  triangle form a finite-state system that must fire the cells near the left
  border exactly when it reaches them; hence the input anti-diagonals cannot
  be periodic over long stretches near the left border (\cref{lem:left}),
  and the half-line cannot be periodic in any wedge at the border
  (\cref{thm:left}); in particular it is never eventually regular, i.e.\
  it cannot consist, from some time on, of finitely many periodic regions
  separated by signals (\cref{cor:regular}).
\item \emph{A validated encoding and a certified four-state bound}
  (\cref{sec:encoding,sec:four}). We encode the bounded problem, together
  with the consequences above, into propositional logic, validate the
  encoding against Mazoyer's solution extracted mechanically from Duprat's
  Coq formalization \cite{Duprat97}, and obtain an LRAT certificate, checked
  in $6$ seconds, that no four-state minimal-time solution exists
  (\cref{thm:four}); the statement does not need the left quiescence
  condition $\delta(\ast,\mathsf L,\mathsf L)=\mathsf L$.
\item \emph{The five-state frontier} (\cref{sec:five}). We exhibit
  explicit five-state rules that satisfy all pumping constraints up to
  length $40$, never fire on the half-line up to anti-diagonal $78$ and
  synchronize every line of length at most $13$ in minimal time; one of them
  ends with a uniform configuration for every length up to $12$
  (\cref{thm:frontier}). Their common half-line, however, is excluded by
  \cref{lem:left} at length $518$ (\cref{cor:germ12}). A further rule,
  $\delta_{14}$, synchronizes every line of length at most $14$; its
  half-line is chaotic and escapes both barriers (\cref{prop:chaotic}). So
  every refutation of five-state solutions along these lines must use lines
  of length at least~$15$. Combining the two
  lemmas with certified solver calls, we classify the half-lines of low
  complexity: every five-state solution uses at least $15$ distinct
  neighbourhoods on its half-line below anti-diagonal $78$
  (\cref{prop:germs}), and of the $9{,}787$ half-lines with at most $16$
  neighbourhoods only $16$, none of them eventually regular, survive. The difficulty lies in the half-line: once the
  transitions used on the half-line are fixed, the completion problem is
  usually decided in under a second, whereas random-probe estimates of the
  natural look-ahead search tree grow from about $27$ nodes for four states
  to $10^{11}$--$10^{19}$ for five.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Related work}
Surveys of the FSSP are \cite{Mazoyer86,Umeo09}; a classical textbook
exposition is \cite{Minsky67}. Mazoyer \cite{Mazoyer96} studied the
structure of optimal solutions and the information flow they require;
Settle and Simon \cite{SettleSimon02} obtained small non-minimal-time
solutions. Beyond Balzer and Sanders,
lower bounds on the number of states are known for variants: no three-state
solution and no four-state symmetric minimal-time solution exist for rings
\cite{BerthiaumeEtAl04}; for further restricted classes see
\cite{Correa17}. Computer searches have been used to find new
solutions \cite{Clergue18} and to generate families of solutions by local
simulations \cite{NguyenMaignan20}. SAT solvers have been applied to many
extremal problems in combinatorics with certified unsatisfiability proofs
\cite{HeuleKullmannMarek16,Heule18}; we are not aware of previous SAT-based work on the state
complexity of the FSSP.
